description: Estonian surnames, frequency-ordered, with counts on the scale of the population register. Two facts shape this list. FIRST, the forest at the top is not decoration: Estonian peasants had no hereditary surnames until the Baltic German landlords assigned them in 1816-1835, and the clerks handed out nature words wholesale — Tamm is oak, Saar ash, Kask birch, Kuusk spruce, Pärn linden, Mänd pine, Lepp alder — so the ten commonest Estonian surnames read like a walk through a wood, and Sepp, the third commonest, simply means smith. A second wave in the 1930s Estonianised German-sounding names by state campaign, which is why the Peterson-Jakobson-Johanson block sits lower down than it once did and why so many families changed to a nature word by choice. SECOND, ESTONIAN SURNAMES DO NOT INFLECT FOR SEX. Tamm is Tamm for a woman and for a man, so there is no person/female/lastName.txt in this pack and its absence is deliberate rather than an omission — unlike Polish, Czech, Latvian or Lithuanian, Estonian has no grammatical gender at all and nothing in a name agrees with the bearer. An Estonian carries one surname. The distribution is genuinely flat: Tamm, the commonest, is carried by roughly five thousand people out of 1.3 million, well under half a per cent, and there is no Estonian equivalent of the Swedish -sson or the Vietnamese Nguyen concentration. What this list deliberately does NOT contain: the Russian surnames of Estonia. About a quarter of the country speaks Russian at home and Ivanov, Smirnov and Vassiljev are among the commonest surnames in Estonia counted as a country — but they are Russian-language names, and blending them into an Estonian-language frequency list would mean guessing proportions. A dataset that needs a realistic Estonia should draw from this pack and the ru pack at roughly 75/25.
