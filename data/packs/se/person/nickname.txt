description: Calling names, and there are three kinds here. The first are the everyday short forms of given names — Áillu, Jovnna, Máret, Ovllá — which in most of Sápmi are simply what a person is called, while the longer registered form sits unused on a document. The second are diminutives in -š: Juhánaš is little Juhán, and the same suffix makes nieiddaš a little girl out of nieida, so it is a live piece of grammar rather than a name-specific ending. The third is the one that matters, because it solves a real problem. Surnames in a Sami village are few and repeat constantly — a school class can hold four unrelated children named Sara — so a person is identified instead by a byname, and the commonest byname is the chain of ancestors' given names: Ovllá-Ánte is the Ánte who belongs to Ovllá, and the chain can run three or four names deep and is the address people actually use, in speech and often in print. The rest are descriptive: stuorra big, uhca little, boares old, nuorra young, guhkes long, vilges white, rukses red, čáhppes black, prefixed to the given name. Some are earned by what a person does — Suohpan-Ánde is Ánde of the lasso, Duodje-Máret is Máret who makes things — and one lives on in the wider world, since the poet and joiker Nils-Aslak Valkeapää published as Áillohaš, which is exactly this pattern.
