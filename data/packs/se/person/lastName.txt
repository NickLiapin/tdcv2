description: Family names across Sápmi, and the list is layered because the history is. The oldest layer is the Sami surnames proper — Sara, Eira, Hætta, Gaup, Utsi, Somby, Buljo, Oskal, Guttorm, Bals, Turi in Norway; Länsman, Magga, Aikio, Valkeapää, Näkkäläjärvi, Paltto, Morottaja in Finland; Labba, Skum, Blind, Pittja, Nutti, Kuhmunen, Svonni, Omma, Marainen, Idivuoma in Sweden. These are heavily concentrated: a school class in Guovdageaidnu can hold four unrelated children named Sara, which is exactly why the byname and the ancestor-name chain do the work of telling people apart in daily life. The second layer is Nordic patronymic surnames — Hansen, Persen, Nilsen, Larsson, Eriksson — carried by Sami families who took or were given them during Norwegianisation and its Swedish equivalent, when a Sami name was a liability at a bank, a school or a land office; a person with Nilsen on a passport is not thereby less Sami, and a dataset that treats a Sami-looking surname as the marker of Sami identity will be wrong about a large share of the population. The third is the Skolt Sami names of the Inari area — Semenoff, Fofonoff, Moshnikoff, Gauriloff — which look Russian because the Skolt Sami are Orthodox, baptised in the Russian church, and were moved into Finland when the Petsamo area was ceded in 1944. The last layer is South Sami: Jåma, Kappfjell, Fjellheim, Bientie, Dunfjeld, from a language area a thousand kilometres south of here whose speakers cannot converse with a Northern Sami speaker. THE WEIGHTS ARE AN ORDERING, NOT MEASURED SHARES. Norway, Sweden and Finland do not publish surname counts by ethnicity and are in most cases legally barred from collecting them, so the curve encodes observed commonness in Sami sources and is set steep, roughly twenty-seven to one from head to tail, because a handful of these names really do cover a very large share of the Northern Sami area.
