description: Malian male given names. Mali is about 95 percent Muslim, so the stock is overwhelmingly Arabic — but it arrives through Manding phonology and French spelling and comes out barely recognisable: Muhammad yields Mamadou, Mahamadou, Mahamane, Mohamed, Mahamar and the clipped Madou, all present here and all the same underlying name; Umar yields Oumar and Oumarou; Ali yields Aly and Aliou; Ibrahim yields Ibrahima, Brahima and Bréhima. A config that deduplicates on string similarity will merge names Malians hold to be distinct people. Underneath sits a Bamanan layer older than the conversion and still given today: Ngolo, Da, Monzon and Biton are Ségou royal names, Nianankoro and Fakoli come straight out of the epics, and Bakoroba, Nampé, Nianzan and Tiéman are ordinary village names with no Arabic in them. Two habits explain why the same handful of names recurs so densely inside one family. A first son is normally named for his grandfather, so a compound can hold three living men with one name and no numeral to separate them — which is what person/suffix is for. And circumstance names are given outright: a child born on Friday may be called Juma, and twins are Sinba and Sina by birth order rather than by choice. The name in this field may then be the one thing nobody says aloud, because an adult is commonly addressed by a teknonym instead — a parent becomes Ba plus the eldest child's name. Adama is a man's name and a woman's name alike and appears in both files, so given names cannot be used to sort rows by sex. Jean, Pierre, Antoine and Augustin belong to the small Christian minority, and Ogobara and Ogotemmêli are Dogon. Weights are an ordering, not measured shares — Mali publishes no name counts — and the decay is gentler than the surname file's, because given names really are spread more widely than jamuw.
