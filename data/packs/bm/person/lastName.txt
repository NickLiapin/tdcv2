description: Malian family names — the jamu — and the first thing to know is how few there are. Mali runs on a short, closed, nationally shared stock: twenty names (Traoré, Keïta, Coulibaly, Diarra, Konaté, Sidibé, Cissé, Touré, Doumbia, Kanté, Sanogo, Diallo, Camara, Dembélé, Sangaré, Bagayoko, Maïga, Fofana, Sacko, Kouyaté) cover an enormous share of the country, and an order of magnitude fewer distinct surnames circulate here than in a European state of the same size. A jamu is not a family name in the European sense at all: it marks a clan and a position in a very old order, so unrelated strangers share one constantly and two people who meet with the same jamu treat each other as kin on that basis alone. Spelling is the one exception in this pack — everywhere else Bambara orthography rules, but a jamu is written the French way, exactly as Malian identity documents write it, accents intact: Traoré with the acute, Keïta and Maïga with the diaeresis. Strip those to bare ASCII and no Malian register would accept the result. Sanankuya, joking kinship, pairs particular jamuw and whole peoples — a Coulibaly and a Keïta, a Traoré and a Diarra, a Bozo fisherman and a Dogon farmer will meet as strangers and immediately trade accusations of bean-eating and servility, and the same bond obliges them to shelter and feed each other and forbids them to spill each other's blood. It is not folklore: Malian mediators invoke it in earnest in real disputes, up to national ones. Inside the list sits a caste layer, the nyamakala — Kouyaté, Diabaté, Sissoko and Soumano are jeli names, the hereditary praise-singers and genealogists, while Kanté, Fané and Ballo are numu, blacksmiths. And Malian surnames do NOT inflect for gender: Aminata Traoré and her brother Amadou Traoré carry the identical string, and a woman keeps her father's jamu when she marries, which is why this pack deliberately has no person/female/lastName.txt. THE WEIGHTS ARE AN ORDERING, NOT MEASURED SHARES. Mali publishes no name frequencies and no census table counts them, so the ranks encode observed commonness and nothing more. The curve is set far steeper than the pack default, about twenty-seven to one from head to tail, because Malian surnames really are concentrated in a way Sindhi or Serbian ones are not.
