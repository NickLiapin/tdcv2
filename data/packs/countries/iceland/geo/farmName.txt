address: iceland.geo.farmName
description: Icelandic rural addresses — the names of farms and country places, from the Dreifbýli (rural delivery) postal codes of Iceland Post's street register, kept where the same name occurs in at least two separate rural codes and weighted by how many it occurs in. THIS IS A WHOLE ADDRESS AND NOT A STREET: outside the towns an Icelandic address has no street and no house number, only the name of the place and the postal code, so a full rural address is two lines — "Hvammur" and "311 Borgarnesi" — and a form that demands street, number and town cannot hold any of the roughly 18,000 people who live this way, one Icelander in twenty-two. THE REPETITION IS THE POINT AND IT IS WHY THE WEIGHT IS THE SPREAD: these names describe the ground, so the same ground gets the same name everywhere — Hvammur (grassy hollow) is a rural address in 34 different postal codes, Hlíð (slope) in 27, Hóll (knoll) in 21, Brekka, Bakki, Grund, Holt, Ás and Hof in a score each. That means a rural Icelandic address is NOT unique without its postal code, which is a property no urban address model expects, and a join on the place name alone will merge farms a hundred kilometres apart. The vocabulary is the oldest layer of Icelandic geography and most of it is unchanged since the settlement: -staðir (stead), -tunga (tongue of land between rivers), -nes (headland), -gerði (enclosure), -kot (cottage), -sel (summer shieling), -á and -vík. Farm names once did the work a surname does elsewhere, because Icelandic patronymics identify a father and not a family: a person was known by the place they farmed. Names carrying a numeral (Bakki 2, Helgafell 1) are farms split between heirs and are left out here rather than emitted without the sibling they contrast with.
locale: is
weighted: true
