description: The two Bhutanese money units. The currency is the ngultrum (ISO 4217 BTN, written Nu. before the figure), divided into 100 chhertum. The name is itself a description of the arrangement: ngul is Dzongkha for silver and trum is borrowed from the Hindi for money. THE FACT THAT GOVERNS EVERYTHING ELSE: the ngultrum has been pegged one to one to the INDIAN RUPEE since it was introduced in 1974, and the rupee circulates freely as legal tender inside Bhutan alongside it. So a Bhutanese till, a Bhutanese price list and a Bhutanese bank account can all be settled in either currency at par, and a shop in Phuentsholing will take rupee notes without comment. Two consequences for anyone modelling Bhutanese money. First, BTN is not an independently floating currency: it moves against the dollar exactly as the rupee moves, so a system holding an INR-BTN rate holds the constant 1, and a system that fetches BTN from a market feed is fetching a derived number. Second, the reverse is NOT true — the ngultrum is not legal tender in India and is not readily exchangeable outside Bhutan, so the peg is symmetrical in rate and asymmetrical in acceptance, and a traveller leaving Bhutan with ngultrum has a souvenir. Indian 500 and 2000 rupee notes were excluded for a period after India's 2016 demonetisation, which is the sort of shock a pegged economy imports whole. Notes in circulation are Nu. 1, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 500 and 1000, issued by the Royal Monetary Authority and carrying the kings on the face; coins are 5, 10, 25 and 50 chhertum and Nu. 1, though chhertum coins are effectively out of daily use. See bhutan.finance.amount for how a figure is printed.
