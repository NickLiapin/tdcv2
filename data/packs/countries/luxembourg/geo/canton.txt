description: The twelve cantons of Luxembourg, the country's only surviving intermediate geography — and a geography with no government in it. A canton has no council, no budget, no elected body and no administration of its own: it is a statistical and electoral unit that groups communes, dating from the French administrative reorganisation of 1795 and kept ever since because the census, the judiciary and the electoral law all needed a level between the commune and the state. Above them there used to be three DISTRICTS — Luxembourg, Diekirch and Grevenmacher — each with a district commissioner; they were ABOLISHED on 3 October 2015 and a Luxembourgish dataset that still has a district column is at least a decade old. Below them are the 100 communes in luxembourg.geo.commune, and that is the only tier that governs anything. The cantons are also the constituencies' building blocks but not the constituencies themselves: elections to the Chamber of Deputies use FOUR circonscriptions — Sud, Centre, Nord and Est — each made of whole cantons, so a canton maps to a constituency and a constituency never maps back to one canton. The sizes are wildly uneven and always have been: Luxembourg and Esch-sur-Alzette hold 31% and 29% of the country's residents between them — nearly six in ten — while Vianden has 5,808 people in three communes, less than one per cent. Names are the official French forms and coincide with the town each canton is named after, which is why "Diekirch" in a Luxembourgish record may be a town, a commune or a canton and only the column heading tells you which. Source: Administration du cadastre et de la topographie, Registre national des localités et des rues, table CANTON.
