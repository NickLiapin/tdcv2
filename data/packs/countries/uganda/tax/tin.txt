description: Ugandan TIN — the Uganda Revenue Authority Taxpayer Identification Number, ten digits, no letters, no separator. Uganda runs a SINGLE TIN across every tax it levies — income tax, VAT, PAYE, withholding tax, customs — so a taxpayer has exactly one number and there is no second identifier to look for; see uganda.tax.vat, which is this same number. THE TENTH DIGIT IS A CHECK DIGIT AND THIS FILE DOES NOT COMPUTE IT, which is a deliberate refusal and needs stating. Uganda's own note to the OECD says the first nine digits are a sequentially assigned number and "the final digit serves as a check digit used for validation purposes... generated using a modulus-based algorithm (commonly referred to as a Mod 97 validation mechanism)". That sentence does not describe a reproducible algorithm, and it cannot be taken at face value: a mod-97 residue runs 0 to 96 and does not fit in one decimal digit, so whatever URA does, it is not the ISO 7064 MOD 97-10 that the phrase suggests, and no fuller specification has been published. It was tested rather than assumed. Two REAL TINs published by URA itself in its withholding-tax exemption list — 1002672779 and 1000090677, both company numbers — were fitted against every simple single-digit rule in the usual family: n mod 97 reduced mod 10, its 97- and 98-complements, the plain digit sum, the 10-complement of the digit sum, n mod 11 and its complement, n mod 7, n mod 9, Luhn, and a weights-10-down-to-2 mod 11. EVERY ONE of them fails on 1002672779; neither TIN is Luhn-valid as a whole string. Two published numbers are far too few to reverse-engineer a rule from — a wrong rule would fit both about one time in a hundred by chance — so this file generates a well-formed ten-digit TIN whose tenth digit is drawn, not derived, and a TIN generated here will not survive URA's own TIN validator. A checksum that is merely a plausible modulus of something is worth less than no checksum, because it looks verified. The nine-digit body is not drawn uniformly: real TINs sit in the 10xxxxxxx band, which is the block URA has been issuing from, so drawing 000000000-999999999 would emit numbers outside the issued range. The first digit is widely said to distinguish individuals from non-individuals; the two published numbers here are both companies and both begin 100, so that distinction could not be confirmed and is not modelled.
