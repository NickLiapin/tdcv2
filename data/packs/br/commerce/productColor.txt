description: Colour as a Breton listing writes it, and the file carries the one Celtic colour fact that will quietly break any mapping to hex. Glas is not blue. It is the colour of the sea, of the sky, of growing grass, of a wet slate roof and of a grey horse — a band running from blue through green into pale grey that Breton treats as a single colour, exactly as Welsh glas and Irish glas do, and it is the ordinary unmarked word for all of them. Gwer is green as well, but the green of made things: a painted door, a bottle, a plastic crate, a printed label. So a product listed glas may reach a French buyer as bleu or as vert depending on what the object is, while one listed gwer is only ever vert. Choosing a single hex value for glas is a decision and not a translation, and choosing #0000FF for it is a mistake — the grass is glas too. Two further terms sit outside the French system: gell is a brown-to-tawny with no clean French equivalent, covering hair, cattle and leather at once, and limestra is a purple taken from Latin that Breton has kept while French replaced its own word for the same colour twice over. The metal finishes are the bare metal nouns — aour, arc'hant, kouevr, arem, houarn, dir — because Breton names a finish by naming the metal and inserts no derived adjective in between, so arc'hant on a listing is silver the colour and arc'hant in a bank is money. The long tail is compounded by juxtaposition, a colour plus a thing that is that colour: gwenn-erc'h snow white, du-pod pot black, melen-amann butter yellow, glas-sklent slate blue, louet-granit granite grey, gwer-raden fern green. Those are descriptive compounds on a live and productive pattern rather than fixed dictionary entries, which is the same way teñval, dark, and sklaer, light, are hung on a base term throughout the list.
