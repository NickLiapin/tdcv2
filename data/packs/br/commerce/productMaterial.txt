description: The substance a product is made of, held as a noun rather than an adjective, because of how Breton builds the phrase: the material noun simply follows the object noun with no preposition at all and usually a hyphen between them. Pod-houarn is an iron pot, ti-mein a stone house, botoù-koad wooden shoes, and a table of oak is un daol dero with nothing in it corresponding to French en or Serbian od. The material also softens after a feminine noun, so koad shows up as un daol goad, and any config joining these two files has the same mutation problem the adjective list has. Three entries carry Brittany's economic history rather than just a substance. Lin is flax: Léon and Trégor spun and wove sailcloth for the Spanish and English fleets from the sixteenth century onward, and the profit paid for the parish closes at Saint-Thégonnec, Guimiliau and Pleyben, so the most extravagant village architecture in France was bought with linen. Bezhin is seaweed, cut off the north Finistère shore and burnt for soda and iodine into the twentieth century, and still harvested industrially for alginates around Lannilis and Landerneau; a seaweed harvester is a goémonier in French and the boats are still working. Sklent is roofing slate from the quarries of Maël-Carhaix and Sizun, which is why the older half of this list is grey. Holen is salt, and the Guérande pans on the southern edge of the country are worked by hand to this day. Pri-poazh, baked clay, is the material of Quimper pottery, made at Locmaria since 1690 — its trade name is the French faïence, which is on this list for that reason. The rest of the modern tail is French on purpose: inox, aluminium, polyester, nylon, viscose and silicone have no settled everyday Breton term, a speaker in a shop says the French word, and a made-up Breton one would be worse than the loan. Plastik and kaoutchoug, by contrast, are genuinely naturalised.
