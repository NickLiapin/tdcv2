description: What follows the prefix in a Breton place name. These are bound second elements rather than free words: they are written inside the name (Kervihan, Kernevez, Lokmaria, Plouzane) and not beside it, which is why so many of them are lower case. Four families. The adjectives of colour and size are listed TWICE, once in the dictionary form and once in the soft-mutated form the compound actually takes, because an adjective lenites after the noun it qualifies — gwenn white gives Kerwenn, bras big gives Kervras, bihan small gives Kervihan, koz old gives Kergoz — and both forms are here so that a config can pick the right one instead of guessing; joining a prefix to the radical form silently manufactures a place that no map shows. Second, the abundance suffix -eg, spelled -ec on the French cadastre, makes a place out of a plant: Kelenneg is the holly ground, Bannaleg the broom ground (and a commune of Finistère), Beuzec the box grove, Bezhineg the seaweed ground; this one suffix accounts for a large share of Brittany's settlement names and is the reason so many of them are botany. Third, the saints, who own Breton toponymy outright — a Plou-, Lan-, Tre- or Lok- name is nearly always followed by the parish's founding saint, and several here (Weltas, Kaourintin, Tudi, Gwenole, Ronan, Ared, Sane) are Brittany's own and stand in no Roman calendar, which is exactly why Rome never canonised them and the parishes kept them anyway. Fourth and smallest, the plural -ou, which turns the name into a set: Kerou, the hamlets. The -ou and -où values are the same ending in two orthographies, the map spelling and the unified one. Case is the trap: personal names are capitalised here and common elements are not, because that is what the written names do, but the solid-joining prefixes absorb whatever follows, so a config building Lok plus Maria must fold the case down to Lokmaria itself, and a config building Sant plus Erwan must insert the hyphen and keep the capital.
