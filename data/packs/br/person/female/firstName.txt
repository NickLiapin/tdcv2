description: Female given name as borne in Brittany, written before the surname; the surname itself comes from person/lastName, the SAME file a man's comes from, because Breton surnames are invariable. Two things are worth knowing before drawing from this list. The first is the shape of the Breton names. Anna-Vari is the classic Breton hyphenated double name, and the second element is mutated: Vari is Mari after the soft mutation, exactly as Yann-Vari on the male list, so the hyphen in a Breton double name is a phonological join and not a typographic one. Soazig, Annaig, Katellig and Enorig show the diminutive -ig, which is where the French-looking spellings Soizic and Annick come from — the same suffix heard by a French ear. Several of the names are legendary rather than merely devotional: Azenor is the mother of Saint Budog, set adrift in a barrel in the story and delivered of her son at sea; Trifin is Sainte Trephine, whose husband in the tale is the Breton original behind Bluebeard; Berc'hed is Brigid, carried over from Ireland, and Marc'harid is Marguerite — both keeping the c'h that no French spelling of them has. The second thing is that the right to use any of these is recent. Under the loi du 11 germinal an XI a French registrar could accept only names from the calendar of saints or from ancient history, which is precisely how Breton names were excluded for a century and a half; the 1966 circular that followed the affaire Le Goarnig admitted a short official schedule of Breton names and no more, so a name not on the schedule was still refusable; and free choice of a first name only arrived with the law of 8 January 1993, which left the registrar with nothing but the child's interest to object with. That is within the lifetime of most women on this list. Alongside the Breton names sit the ordinary French ones — Marie, Anne, Yvonne, Françoise, Jeanne, Monique, Nathalie, Sylvie — because they are what most women in Brittany are actually called, and the hybrid forms Annick, Soizic, Gaëlle, Armelle, Maryvonne and Marie-Annick are the most Breton thing in the file: French morphology on a Breton root, and the commonest way the region signs its name.
