description: Language names in Breton, and the file turns on one suffix: -eg, which makes a language out of a country or a people and is the same suffix the language wears on itself — Breizh gives brezhoneg, Saoz the Saxon gives saozneg, Kembre gives kembraeg, Iwerzhon gives iwerzhoneg, Kernev-Veur which is Cornwall gives kerneveureg, and after that alamaneg, spagnoleg, italianeg, sinaeg and japaneg follow without exception. The names are lowercase, as Breton and French both write them and English does not. The list is honest about where brezhoneg actually stands. Roughly two hundred thousand people speak it, against more than a million a century ago — a loss of four fifths inside four generations, and one of the sharpest declines recorded for any language in western Europe. What remains is overwhelmingly elderly, well over half of it past sixty-five, because household transmission largely stopped in the middle of the twentieth century, when a generation of parents concluded, under pressure they did not invent, that their children would get on better in French. Every one of those speakers also speaks French, and uses French with the mairie, the tax office, the doctor, the school and the employer without exception: there is no monolingual Breton speaker left and no domain in which Breton is the language anybody must use. That is why galleg sits second here — not out of courtesy, but because it is the other language of every single person the first value describes. Gallaoueg is third and is the value most often got wrong. Gallo is the Romance language of Upper Brittany, a langue d'oïl and a sister of Norman and Picard, and it is neither a dialect of French nor a variety of Breton; its own speakers called it patois for generations and only named it Gallo as they began to defend it, and it still has no single agreed spelling, three rival systems having been put forward since the 1970s in the same argument Breton had over peurunvan, fought thirty years later and on a smaller stage. The Breton block runs as far as the languages for which Breton has a settled -eg form. The tail is deliberately FRENCH — berbère, darija, wolof, bambara, soninké, peul, lingala, comorien, malgache, tamoul, ourdou, pachto, dari and the rest — because those are the languages Brittany's immigrant families and the African and Asian intakes of the universities at Rennes and Brest really bring, and Breton has no settled name for most of them; a coined -eg would be worse than the French word a Breton speaker actually uses when he names his neighbour's language.
