description: Nationality, and the first thing to know is that Breton does not do here what French does. French has one word carrying every job — un Français, la langue française, la cuisine française — while Breton, like Welsh and Irish, has no nationality adjective at all: it builds the person on the country name with the suffix -ad, and swaps to -iz for the plural. So Breizhad is a Breton and Breizhiz are the Bretons; Kembread and Kembreiz; Leonad and Leoniz for a man of the Léon. The oldest names are irregular and take no suffix — Gall is a Frenchman, plural Gallaoued; Saoz an Englishman, plural Saozon — and where French would reach for the adjective, Breton simply juxtaposes the bare country name instead. A config that treats a value here as an adjective, or that forms a plural by adding -s, will produce something no Breton would write. The opening value is not a nationality in the legal sense and that is exactly the point of putting it first. Breizhad is a cultural identity that no state recognises: France records citizenship and nothing else, the census asks no question about regional belonging, and a Breton's identity card, passport and birth certificate all say française — a person can be Breizhad every day of his life and never once be it on paper. Nor does the identity line up with the administrative region, which leaves out Loire-Atlantique and with it Naoned, Nantes, the historic capital of the duchy. The Breton block that follows is short on purpose. It holds the Celtic peoples, whom Breton has always needed to tell apart and names anciently and irregularly, and then the European and world peoples for which the regular -ad formation on the Breton country name is in current use. Everything from Autrichien onward is deliberately FRENCH, in the capitalised noun form French uses for a person (un Marocain, une Marocaine), because Breton has no settled demonym for most of these countries and a coined -ad would be an invention rather than a word — the French form is what a Breton speaker actually says. The African and Indian Ocean entries are on the list because those are the communities really present in Rennes, Brest and Lorient, not because the atlas needed covering. Note too that the French forms inflect for sex — Marocain against Marocaine, Sénégalais against Sénégalaise — so a config pairing one of them with a female row must add the -e itself; the Breton -ad forms do not inflect that way and take the separate feminine suffix -ez instead.
