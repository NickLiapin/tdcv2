description: Male given name as actually borne in Brittany, written before the surname. The list is two populations in one and that is deliberate. The head is Breton — Yann, Erwan, Ronan, Gwenaël, Tanguy, Youenn, Fañch, Maël, Korentin, Goulven, Efflam, Judikael — and most of those are saints of the pardons, the parish pilgrimages that still fill a church and a field once a year: Korentin is the first bishop of Kemper, Goulven, Efflam, Sezni, Tudi, Maodez and Jaoua each have a parish and usually a holy well named after them, and the seven founders of the Tro Breizh (Paol, Tugdual, Brieg, Malo, Samzun, Padern, Kaourintin) appear here as ordinary living first names, so a Breton boy is routinely given the name of a fifth-century monk who arrived from Wales or Ireland by sea. The rest is plain French — Jean, Pierre, Michel, Yves, Louis, Jacques, Christophe, Sébastien — and it belongs here, because the population of Brittany is French too and a list that is a hundred per cent militant Breton is not Brittany. Yves is the hinge: it is Erwan in Breton, the same thirteenth-century lawyer-priest of Landreger who is patron of Brittany and of advocates, so a family choosing between Yves and Erwan is choosing a language and not a saint. The compounds are specific and real: Jean-Yves, Pierre-Yves and Yves-Marie in French, Yann-Ber, Yann-Vari, Yann-Fañch and Per-Jakez in Breton, where the second element mutates — Vari is Mari and Ber is Pêr, both softened by the join. Yannig, Perig and Lomig carry the diminutive -ig, still fully productive; a French ear wrote that ending as -ick, which is where Yannick comes from, and those spellings are now full legal names in their own right. NONE of this passed without a fight. Within living memory a French registrar could refuse a Breton name outright: in the affaire Le Goarnig, in the 1960s, the children of a Finistère family were held to have no legal existence at all because their given names were not on the state's admitted list, and it took a circular in 1966 to open that list to a schedule of Breton names. The argument has never quite closed — a boy registered as Fañch at Kemper in 2017 was taken to court by the prosecutor over the tilde alone, on the ground that ñ was not a letter of the French language; the parents lost at first instance, won before the Rennes court of appeal in 2018, and the Cour de cassation let that judgment stand in 2019. The tilde in this file is a legal fact as much as an orthographic one. THE WEIGHTS ARE AN ORDERING, NOT MEASURED SHARES: France publishes births by given name nationally but not in a regional form that isolates Brittany, so the counts are a rank-decay curve, 1000000 divided by rank to the power 0.55, a shallow slope of about nineteen to one, chosen because given names genuinely are spread — no single one of these covers a large share of anybody.
