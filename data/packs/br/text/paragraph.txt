description: Filler paragraphs in Breton, three or four sentences each, ordinary in subject throughout — a house at first light, a boat leaving before the day, the Monday market at Landerne, a fest-noz in the parish hall, rain on Menez Are, the school run, cider poured into a bowl beside the crêpes, a pardon walking out to its chapel, the coach to Roazhon, a Saturday football match — and none of it is a lesson about Breton. What a paragraph shows and a single sentence cannot is first the joinery: ha 'and' (hag before a vowel), met 'but', pe 'or', rak 'because', neuze 'then, so', goude 'after', memes tra 'all the same', and above all ma 'if', which pulls a mixed mutation onto the verb behind it — ma teu glav e vo diaes, 'if rain comes it will be hard', where the stem of dont is deu and is written teu. The second is the sentence frame that makes Breton word order look like nothing else spoken in France: one element is thrown to the front and a bare particle then stands between it and the verb, a when the fronted element is the subject and e when it is anything else. Ar vugale a gan er sal fronts the children, the subject, hence a; Er sal e kan ar vugale fronts the room, so the particle is e. The particle is not decoration, because it mutates what follows it: a lenites (kanañ gives a gan, debriñ gives a zebr, gwelout gives a wel) while e takes the mixed mutation (bez gives e vez, deu gives e teu), so one verb wears two different first letters according only to what was put in front of it. Three further things are worth knowing before these lines are parsed. A full noun subject leaves the verb in the third person SINGULAR however many people it names — ar vugale a zo o c'hoari, 'the children are playing', never a plural verb — and the plural ending surfaces only where no noun subject stands in that slot, kontañ a reont, 'they tell'. The verb to be splits three ways: eo for identity and predicates, zo for existence, emañ for being located somewhere, so that Skuizh eo ma zad, Ur vag a zo er porzh and Emañ ma zad er park are one English verb wearing three Breton shapes. Reported speech runs through eme, an invariable quotative that puts its speaker after it — eme ma zad, 'said my father', emezi, 'said she' — beside the plainer lavarout e ... for the indirect version, and the relative clause uses that same bare a and e with no relative pronoun at all, ur pesketaer a oa o labourat war e vag, 'a fisherman who was working on his boat', which is why a Breton relative and a fronted main clause are the same shape on the page. Place names stand in their Breton form here — Roazhon, Landerne, Kemper, Gwengamp, Konk-Kerne, An Oriant, Enez-Sun, Menez Are — though the French twin of each is in daily use beside it and a dataset drawn from Brittany will meet both.
