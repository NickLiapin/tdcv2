description: Filler words in Breton, the lorem-ipsum vocabulary of this pack, gathered by hand rather than cut out of a corpus: the spread is deliberately ordinary — kin and the household, the body, the farm and the sea, food and drink, animals and plants, the village and the town, weather and tide, work and trade, the church and the school, time, abstractions, adjectives, verbs, numerals and the function words — so that a random draw reads as Breton and not as one subject's glossary. Every value here is a DICTIONARY form, and that is the trap: Breton changes a word's first consonant according to what stands in front of it, so a generator that drops one of these into running text after ma, da, ar, daou or tri produces a form that is wrong in that slot — tad 'father' is ma zad 'my father' under the spirant mutation but da dad 'your father' under the soft one, penn is ma fenn and da benn, ki is ma c'hi and da gi, and not one of those six strings is in this file. Verbs stand in the bare stem — debr, ev, gwel, kouezh, sav, gwerzh — because Breton has no infinitive ending to cite them by: the citation form is built with -añ, -iñ or -out (debriñ, kanañ, gwelout) and the verbal noun does the work an infinitive does elsewhere, which is why kan is a song and the stem of to sing at once, and labour, soñj and c'hoari are each a noun and a verb without changing shape. The orthography is peurunvan, the unified spelling agreed in 1941 and still known as the orthographe ZH, which writes zh at exactly the points where the KLT dialects say /z/ and Vannetais says /h/ — kazh, laezh, gwinizh, bezhin, brezhoneg, traezh — so that Breizh is read Breiz in the west and Breih in the south-east, one digraph carrying a dialect split and a compromise between two literary traditions that had until then written apart. c'h is a single letter of the alphabet, a velar fricative, and the apostrophe belongs inside the letter rather than being punctuation between two others: marc'h, kerc'h, erc'h, buoc'h, peoc'h, pelec'h and c'hwec'h must never be split across it, stripped of it, or normalised away, and c'h is not the letter ch. The numerals are not a decimal series, because Breton counts by twenties: ugent is 20, daou-ugent 'two twenties' is 40, tri-ugent is 60 and pevar-ugent is 80, with hanter-kant 'half a hundred' sitting oddly at 50 in the middle of them, and 18 is triwec'h, 'three sixes'. Settled French loans belong here and are not a lapse — kafe, patatez, karotez, ognon, familh, kazetenn, ostaleri, kastell — because they are what a speaker actually says. A word carrying two senses appears once and once only: amzer is both the weather and time, mare is the tide and the season, eost is the harvest and the month of August, gouel is a sail and a feast day, lenn is a pond and the stem of to read, and te is tea and the pronoun thou.
