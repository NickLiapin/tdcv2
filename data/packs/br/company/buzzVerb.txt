description: Buzz verbs, and the French ones are in the infinitive, which is the form a slogan uses: French business prose is full of bare infinitives standing as headings - Optimiser vos flux, Accompagner la transition - so these values drop straight into a catch-phrase without conjugation, and a config that needs a finite verb must inflect them itself. The English-derived block shows how the borrowing actually works in French offices: an English stem takes the regular first-conjugation ending -er and is then treated as a French verb in every respect, so booster, scaler, benchmarker and pitcher conjugate as boostons, scalé, benchmarkait, and digitaliser is a French verb built on an English root that English itself does not have in that shape. Two verbs in the French block are regionally loaded rather than generic. Mutualiser is not corporate padding in Brittany: it is what a CUMA does with a combine harvester and what a co-operative does with a cold store, and the word carries a century of farmers' mutual-aid practice behind it. Fédérer is the vocabulary of the association and the federation, the legal shape most Breton cultural and language activity takes. The six Breton verbs at the end are given as verb-nouns, which is the citation form Breton dictionaries use and the form Breton puts where French puts an infinitive - the language has no separate infinitive, and what looks like one is a noun: gwerzhañ is selling and the selling, kreskiñ growing, kenlabourat working together, aozañ organising, sevel raising or setting up, diorren developing. The -añ and -iñ endings are the two productive verb-noun suffixes and the choice between them is lexical, not a rule a generator can apply. As everywhere in this pack, they are dictionary forms: sevel after the particle da becomes da sevel unchanged, but gwerzhañ after da becomes da werzhañ, gw softening to w, so dropping one of these into a Breton phrase unmutated will be visibly wrong.
