description: Spices and herbs. Ai-manas leads and is worth reading literally: ai is tree or plant, manas is hot, so chilli is the hot plant — a transparent compound made on the island for something the Portuguese brought from the other side of the world, which is how you can tell it entered through the garden rather than through the church. Lia ginger, laos galangal, kunyit turmeric and serewalla lemongrass are the Malay-Austronesian rhizome-and-grass set that a Timorese cook actually uses daily; serewalla is often shortened to sere in speech and the short form is not in this list because the long one is what a written recipe carries. Noz moskada and kravu, nutmeg and cloves, matter historically: Timor sits at the end of the same trade route that ran through Banda and Ternate, and the spice ships that made the eastern archipelago famous were the reason European traders were in these waters at all — though what they came to Timor for was not spice but sandalwood. The rest is Portuguese put through the spelling rules, and asafraun from açafrão does two of them at once: ç is written s and the nasal ending -ão is written -aun. Kominu, tomillu, funxu and manjerikaun show the same machinery on cominho, tomilho, funcho and manjericão.
