description: Familiar names, and three quite different things are on the list. The first is the ordinary Portuguese hypocoristic reshaped by Tetum sound rules, which is why the short forms look unlike their Portuguese cousins: Francisco gives Xiku and Kiku rather than Chico, because Portuguese ch is written x in Tetum and c before a back vowel is written k; Domingos gives Mingu and Domi, with the unstressed final -o landing as -u exactly as it does in obrigado to obrigadu; José gives Zé, Maria gives Mari and Mia, Agostinho gives Tinu, Filomena gives Mena, Angelina gives Lina, Sebastião gives Bastião and Basti, Januário gives Nariu, Aleixo gives Lexu and Abílio gives Biliu. The second is the Timorese layer, which is not Portuguese at all: Mau, Bere, Buti, Loro, Lelo, Nai, Bui, Kiik (small), Boot (big), Meta (black), Mutin (white), Rai (land), Funu and Ukun are Tetum words used as everyday names, and they attach to a person independently of whatever the baptismal register holds, so a nickname column here must NOT be derived from the first-name column. Behind that layer sits the resistance nom de guerre, a genuine institution rather than a curiosity: a Falintil fighter took a war name and many kept it for life and are known by it publicly to this day, which is how Xanana, Lú-Olo, Taur, Lere, Kilik and Mauk came to be names ordinary people answer to and, in Xanana's case, to be given to newborns. The third is kinship words used to address people who are not relatives. Maun is elder brother and Bin elder sister, and both are the normal polite way to address a slightly older stranger in Dili - a market seller, a colleague, a driver - so they function as address terms rather than as names, and Kaka does the same job in the Indonesian-inherited register left by the occupation. A dataset using this file as a display name should expect all three kinds to appear where a Western nickname column would carry only the first.
locale: tet
