description: Leading element of a Timorese place name. This is not a Portuguese morphology with local words dropped into it — it is the real one, and it is transparent in a way European toponymy usually is not. Timorese place names are legible sentences about the ground they stand on, so the first element is nearly always a noun of terrain and the second says something about it: Bebonuk, Becora and Beaço all open with the word for water; Fatuhada, Fatubessi and Fatumean open with stone; Aileu, Ainaro and Aimutin open with tree or wood; Mota is river, and Mota Ain, foot of the river, is the name of the land border crossing into Indonesian West Timor. A name is therefore evidence. If a settlement begins with Bee or Be there is a spring or a stream at it, and if it begins with Fatu there is rock, which is why the same elements recur across languages that are otherwise unrelated — Tetum, Mambai, Makasae, Bunak and Fataluku all name places this way and the country has around thirty languages doing it. Hato, Lau, Lo, Mau, Ues, Uai, Bau, Ata, Leo and Beto are the other high-frequency openers, recoverable from Hatolia, Hatobuilico, Laulara, Lospalos, Maubisse, Maubara, Uatolari, Uatucarbau, Baucau, Atsabe and Betano; several of them are opaque even to speakers now, which is the ordinary fate of an element that has been a place name for longer than it has been a word. The Portuguese layer — Vila, Santa, Santu, Saun, Nova, Portu, Sidade, Ponta, Kosta and Illa, the last from ilha by the lh-to-ll rule — is the colonial overlay and sits on administrative centres and missions rather than on villages. Kampung and Kota are the Indonesian occupation's contribution and survive in Dili, where Kampung Alor is still called that.
locale: tet
