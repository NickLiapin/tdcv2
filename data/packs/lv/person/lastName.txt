description: Latvian surname in the MASCULINE form. Latvian surnames inflect for sex: the masculine ends in -s or -š (Bērziņš, Ozols, Kalniņš) and the feminine drops it for -a or -e (Bērziņa, Ozola, Kalniņa; Balodis becomes Balode, Eglītis becomes Eglīte, Kaminskis becomes Kaminska). So a female row must draw from person/female/lastName.txt, which is this same list derived line for line, and never from this one. A minority of surnames — Priede, Lapsa, Egle, Roze, Caune, Skuja, Straume, Vīksne, Kalve, Lauva — already end in -a or -e and are identical for both sexes; that is a real category, not a gap. EVERY name in a Latvian record is Latvianised, including foreign ones, and this is the single most distinctive thing about Latvian naming. Latvian law requires a foreign surname to be written in Latvian script with a Latvian ending, so George Bush is entered as Džordžs Bušs, Barack Obama as Baraks Obama, Bill Clinton as Bils Klintons and William Shakespeare as Viljams Šekspīrs — in passports, in court judgments and in the newspaper. No other European language does this so systematically, and it is why the Russian-origin surnames at the tail of this list all carry a Latvian -s (Ivanovs, Petrovs, Kuzņecovs) rather than the bare Russian form, and why their feminine forms are Ivanova and Petrova by the same Latvian rule that gives Bērziņa. A Latvian citizen may request the unmodified original in a second line of the passport, but the Latvianised form is the legal one. The order is the real one: Bērziņš leads the Latvian register with roughly 11 000 bearers, followed by Kalniņš, Ozoliņš, Jansons and Ozols. Weights are the pack default rank-decay curve rather than measured counts, because Latvian surnames are genuinely spread — the curve's top share of about 0,35% is close to Bērziņš's real share of about 0,6% and does not misrepresent the country. Note the Latvian decimal comma in those figures.
