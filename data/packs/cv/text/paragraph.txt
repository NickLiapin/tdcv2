They carry three things a single sentence cannot show. The first is the joinery — тата and, анчах but, е or, ҫавӑнпа therefore, унтан then, вара so, ҫапах even so, and the conditional in -сан/-сен that opens a great many ordinary Chuvash paragraphs: ҫумӑр ҫусан, уя тухмастпӑр, if it rains we do not go out to the field. The second is the participle, because Chuvash has no relative pronoun at all and builds a relative clause by setting a participle in front of the noun, with the whole clause ahead of the thing it describes and no word anywhere answering to which or that: вӑрманта ӳсекен юман the oak that grows in the forest, атте лартнӑ пӳрт the house father built, ӗнер килнӗ хӑнасем the guests who came yesterday. The third is the converb in -са/-се, which hangs one action off another and lets a sentence run on without a second finite verb: ларса вулать reads sitting, тухса кайрӗ went out, пуҫтарса пӗтерчӗ finished gathering. Reported speech is worth watching as well, since the quotative тет or терӗ follows what was said instead of introducing it — вӑл ыран килет, терӗ, he said he would come tomorrow. The subject matter stays deliberately ordinary: a household before first light, a hop garden in August, the ice going out on the Volga, an Акатуй field, a Chuvash lesson in a village school, the evening bus back from Cheboksary. None of it is a lesson about Chuvash.
