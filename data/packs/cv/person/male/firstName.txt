description: Male given name in the Chuvash Republic, built in three visible layers that a single man may occupy all at once. The first layer is the Chuvash form of a Russian baptismal name — Мишши for Mikhail, Ваҫлей for Vasily, Хӗветӗр for Fyodor, Кӗркури for Grigory, Ҫтаппан for Stepan, Петӗр for Pyotr, Микула for Nikolai, Тимахви for Timofey, Энтри for Andrey, Кӗҫтенкке for Konstantin, Яккӑв for Yakov, Уҫӑп for Osip, Хӗлип for Filipp. These are not nicknames. They are what a man is called in the village, what a Chuvash-language newspaper prints, and what goes on a grave marker, and the reshaping is systematic Chuvash phonology rather than affection, which is why Хӗветӗр is barely recognisable as its source. The second layer is the pre-Christian and literary stock, the names that carry the culture: Сетнер above all, and Тӑхтаман, the two men of Konstantin Ivanov's 1908 poem Нарспи, which is the Chuvash national epic; Ҫеҫпӗл, from the poet Mikhail Sespel who made modern Chuvash verse; and the word-names Атӑл (the Volga), Юман (oak), Тимӗр (iron), Улӑп (the giant of the Chuvash hero tales), Асамат (the rainbow), alongside old Turkic-shaped names such as Атнер, Айтар, Пикмӑрса and Ахтупай. The third layer is the plain Russian passport form — Иван, Николай, Александр, Владимир, Пётр, Сергей — and it is here because it is true, not as a failure: a Chuvash man's document name and his village name are routinely two different strings, the same person is Микула to his neighbours and Николай to the pension office, and a dataset that hid that would be describing a country that does not exist. Roughly a quarter of this list is Chuvash, which over-represents the registry and under-represents the village, and sits about where a Chuvash-language text would land. THE WEIGHTS ARE AN ORDERING, NOT MEASURED SHARES, and the curve is much flatter than the surname one because given names really are spread — no single one of these is anywhere near the share that Иванов takes among the surnames.
