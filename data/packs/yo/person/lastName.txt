description: Yoruba family name. ONE list serves both sexes — Yoruba has no grammatical gender and a family name never inflects, which is why there is no person/female/lastName.txt; its absence is the design, not an omission. Two things a config cannot infer. FIRST, the inherited surname is recent. Traditionally a Yoruba person was identified by given name plus father's given name plus the ìdílé and its orílẹ̀ praise-poetry; the fixed heritable surname arrived with colonial registration and church baptism in the 19th and early 20th centuries, and the stock it was built from is the SAME sentence-name stock as the given names. So this list deliberately overlaps person/male/firstName.txt and person/female/firstName.txt — Adébáyọ̀, Ọlátúnjí, Àbíọ́lá, Ìdòwú and Bàbátúndé are each a first name for one person and a surname for another, and reading a Yoruba full name you cannot tell from the words alone which half is which. SECOND, many surnames name the lineage's ÒRÌṢÀ: Ògún- the god of iron, Fá-/Ifá- the oracle, and (not carried here) Ṣàngó-, Ọ̀ṣun-, Oya-. Christian converts routinely swapped that head for Olú-/Olúwa-, God, so Ifátúnjí became Olútúnjí and Ògúndáre Olúdáre — the same family, one letter of theology apart. Balógun is a war-chief title turned surname. Two real groups are NOT in this list and are named here so nobody thinks they were forgotten: the Yorubaised Arabic surnames of Muslim families (Gbádámọ̀ṣí is the one representative kept), and the English and Portuguese surnames of the Saro and Amaro returnees of Lagos — Macaulay, Coker, Savage, Da Silva, Da Rocha — which are real Yoruba family names but are not Yoruba-LANGUAGE words, and a config that wants them can draw en.person.lastName. Weights are rank-derived on the brief's gentle curve; Nigeria publishes no surname frequency table, and the real distribution is very flat because the name stock is productive.
