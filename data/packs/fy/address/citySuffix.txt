description: Trailing element of a Frisian place name. Unlike the Dutch list, which mostly names the water a town stands on and is written as a separate phrase, the Frisian one is morphology: these fuse onto the stem and they are what makes a name recognisable as Frisian at a glance. Two of them carry the province's history. The commonest, -um, is not a word at all but the worn remains of an old dative plural meaning roughly 'at the homes of', which is why Dokkum, Marrum, Berltsum, Bitgum and Hallum are all grammatically 'at the so-and-so's' and why the ending cannot be translated, only recognised. And -terp explains the landscape: a terp is a dwelling mound raised by hand, basket by basket, over centuries, to hold a farmstead and its stock above the storm tides, and the Frisian coast was settled that way for a thousand years before there were dykes to make it unnecessary - which is why the oldest villages in the flattest country in Europe all sit on a hill, and why a Dutch map of Fryslân is dotted with contour rings that no geology explains. The rest are ordinary landscape nouns doing the same work: -gea is a parish or district and is the -ga that Dutch spelling gives these names (Aldegea for Oudega, Wergea for Warga), -wâld a wood, -fean peat, -syl a sluice, -tille a small bridge, -mar a lake (Eastermar, Gaastmar), -hûzen the houses, -bûrren the neighbours, -sweach a strip of cleared land, -brêge a bridge, -kleaster a monastery. A stins was a fortified stone tower house and a state the unfortified country seat that replaced it; both survive as the last element of hamlet names. The final four are compass markers for a district of a town, not a separate town.
